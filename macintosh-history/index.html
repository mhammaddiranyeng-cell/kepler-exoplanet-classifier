    The Macintosh

    A forty-year history of the machine that argued a computer should be something you meet, not something you operate.

    01

    A project named after an apple

    In 1979 Jef Raskin, Apple employee number 31, began arguing for a computer that an ordinary person could buy for a thousand dollars and simply use. He named the project after his favourite variety of apple, the McIntosh, and misspelled it to avoid the hi-fi company of the same name.

    The same year, a delegation from Apple visited Xerox PARC and saw the Alto: overlapping windows, a bitmapped display, a mouse. Xerox had built the future and filed it. Apple went home and started shipping it — first in the expensive, unsuccessful Lisa, then in the machine that mattered.

    By 1981 Steve Jobs had pushed Raskin off his own project and turned it into something faster, prettier and considerably less practical: a sealed box with a graphical interface, driven by a Motorola 68000 that Burrell Smith had coaxed onto a single board.

    02

    January 24, 1984

    Two days earlier, during the third quarter of Super Bowl XVIII, Apple ran a sixty-second film directed by Ridley Scott in which a runner threw a hammer through a screen. It named no product and showed no computer. It aired once nationally and is still being talked about four decades later.

    At the shareholders’ meeting in Flint Center, Jobs pulled the machine from a bag, put a disk in it, and let it introduce itself. The screen drew the word hello in a handwritten script, then scrolled through fonts nobody had seen on a personal computer, and finally the Macintosh spoke: Never trust a computer you can’t lift.

    It cost $2,495, shipped with 128 kilobytes of memory and a single 400K floppy drive, and had no hard disk, no colour, no expansion slots and no cursor keys. Almost every one of those absences was a deliberate argument.

    One machine, nine decisions

    Everything below is generated in code — no downloaded model, no scanned parts. As you descend, the Macintosh 128K comes apart. Turn around and go back up and it puts itself together again, in exactly the reverse order.

    Front bezel

    A face, not a box

    Jerry Manock and Terry Oyama stood the machine upright so its footprint was smaller than a sheet of paper, and set the screen high with the drive below — proportions the team openly described as a face. Every other computer of 1984 lay down like an appliance.

    Cathode ray tube

    512 × 342, square pixels

    Nine inches, one bit deep, and — crucially — pixels that were actually square. That is why a circle on a Mac screen was a circle, why WYSIWYG worked, and why the machine could credibly claim that what you saw was what you would print.

    400K floppy drive

    The one slot that mattered

    Sony’s rigid 3.5-inch disk replaced the flapping 5.25-inch floppy, driven by Woz’s Integrated Woz Machine. With 128K of RAM and one drive, using MacWrite and MacPaint together meant swapping disks until your wrist ached.

    Digital logic board

    An 8 MHz argument

    Burrell Smith’s board carried a Motorola 68000 running at 7.83 MHz, sixteen DRAM chips totalling 128 kilobytes, and 64K of ROM holding the QuickDraw graphics routines Bill Atkinson had written. The ROM was the operating system’s soul, burned into silicon.

    Analog board

    No fan, by decree

    Rod Holt built a switching supply that ran the display and the logic without a cooling fan, because Jobs insisted the Macintosh be silent. It was. It also cooked itself, and the early machines earned the nickname “the beige toaster”.

    Inside the rear housing

    Forty-seven signatures

    In 1982 the team signed a sheet of paper, and the signatures were moulded into the inside of every case — invisible unless you broke the machine open. Artists sign their work. Most owners never saw them; you are seeing them now.

    Keyboard & mouse

    One button, no arrows

    The keyboard shipped without cursor keys, on purpose: if you wanted to move, you reached for the mouse. And the mouse had exactly one button, because two is a question and one is an answer. The industry spent twenty years disagreeing.

    Back together

    $2,495, and a dent in the universe

    Nine parts, closed up again — and reversible the whole way: scroll back up and the machine comes apart in the opposite order. Seventy thousand sold in the first hundred days, then a collapse. The Macintosh was charming, underpowered and nearly fatal to the company. What saved it was not the machine — it was what people started printing with it.

    03

    Saved by a printer

    Sales fell through 1984 and 1985. Jobs lost a boardroom fight with John Sculley and left Apple in September 1985 to found NeXT. The Macintosh was, by most measures, failing.

    Then three things met each other: the LaserWriter, Adobe’s PostScript, and Aldus PageMaker. Suddenly a Mac, a printer and $7,000 could replace a typesetting bureau. An entire industry — desktop publishing — was invented on the strength of it, and it belonged almost entirely to Apple.

    The Macintosh Plus arrived in 1986 with a megabyte of RAM and SCSI, and the machine finally had room to breathe.

    04

    Getting up from the desk

    The 1989 Macintosh Portable weighed sixteen pounds and cost $6,500; it is remembered mostly as a lesson. Two years later the PowerBook 100/140/170 got it right by doing something nobody else had thought to do: push the keyboard back against the hinge and leave a palm rest with a trackball in the middle.

    Every laptop you have ever used copies that layout. Apple sold a billion dollars of PowerBooks in the first year.

    05

    The wilderness years

    Between 1993 and 1997 Apple shipped a Performa for every conceivable retail channel, licensed the Mac OS to clone makers who ate its best margins, and spent years failing to replace an operating system that still had no protected memory and no pre-emptive multitasking.

    The PowerPC transition in 1994 was a genuine engineering triumph — a 68000 emulator good enough that most users never noticed their software was being translated. It bought time that Copland, the doomed next-generation OS, then wasted.

    By 1996 Apple was losing money by the billion and shopping for an operating system. It bought NeXT for $429 million, and got Steve Jobs back in the deal.

    06

    Bondi blue

    Jobs killed the clones, the Newton and most of the product line, and in May 1998 introduced a translucent teardrop with a handle on top. The iMac G3 had no floppy drive — an omission critics called suicidal — and two USB ports, a standard nobody was using yet.

    It sold 800,000 units in its first five months, dragged USB into the mainstream on its own, and re-established that a Macintosh was allowed to be an object with a personality.

    07

    A Unix underneath

    Mac OS X 10.0 shipped in March 2001: NeXTSTEP’s Mach and BSD foundations, Objective-C frameworks, and a candy-coloured interface called Aqua that Jobs said people would want to lick.

    It was slow, incomplete, and unmistakably the right answer. Classic Mac OS applications ran in a compatibility layer while developers moved to Carbon and then Cocoa. Within four years the transition was done and the Macintosh had a modern, protected, multi-user kernel under a genuinely beautiful desktop.

    The hardware caught up in style: the Titanium PowerBook G4 in 2001, the sunflower iMac G4 in 2002, the aluminium Cinema Displays.

    08

    “It has been leading a double life”

    At WWDC 2005 Jobs announced that Mac OS X had been secretly compiled for Intel for five years, and that the Macintosh would leave PowerPC behind. The first Intel Macs arrived in January 2006; the transition finished inside a year.

    Rosetta translated PowerPC software in the meantime, and Boot Camp did something previously unthinkable: it let a Macintosh run Windows natively. For the first time buying a Mac carried no software risk at all, and the Mac’s market share began a decade of climbing.

    The unibody MacBook Pro of 2008 — milled from a single block of aluminium — set the physical template that laptops still follow, and the 2012 Retina display ended the era of visible pixels.

    09

    Back to its own silicon

    In November 2020 the Macintosh changed processor architectures for the third time. The M1 was an iPhone lineage chip with the CPU, GPU and memory on one package, and it made a fanless MacBook Air faster than the Intel machines above it in the price list.

    Rosetta 2 translated x86 binaries ahead of time, well enough that emulated software often outran the native code it replaced. The transition took two years. The line that followed — Pro, Max, Ultra, the Mac Studio, the 24-inch iMac in colours borrowed straight from 1998 — is the most coherent the Macintosh has ever been.

    Forty years on, the argument the 128K made in a sealed beige case has simply become how computers work.
